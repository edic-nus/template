1. Introduction

1.1 Formula SAE Competition

Formula SAE (FSAE) is an intercollegiate student design competition organized by SAE International where university teams from around the world design, manufacture, build, and race formula-style race cars. Cars are judged based on design, cost, efficiency, and performance through a series of dynamic and static events. Team members will also be judged on their capabilities in team management, design thinking, technical knowledge, and engineering practices.

1.2 NUS Formula SAE EV Team and Competition History

National University of Singapore (NUS) FSAE ventured into the Electric Vehicle (EV) category in 2022 and had the highest placing in 2025 with 7th overall. The most recent competition in 2026 saw the team place 39th due to unforeseen technical failures.

In the FSAE Michigan endurance event, corners take up 70% of total distance, a significant portion of the track. Furthermore, the team stands to gain the most points from endurance out of all the events.

3.3 Performance and Simulation Objectives

Another problem we face is that our simulations may not be accurate as the car’s CAD geometry may not match the actual manufactured components.

We aim to improve cornering downforce and aerodynamic efficiency which is measured by the ratio of Coefficient of lift to Coefficient of Drag (CL/CD). Concurrently, we will work on increasing simulation reliability and stability.

4. Problem of Interest

4.1 Asymmetrical Airflow During Cornering

The car experiences asymmetrical airflow when cornering. In corners, the outer radius of the car path is greater than the inner radius, so the outer velocity is greater than the inner velocity. The tires are also in different positions (no longer mirrored) during cornering. This further disrupts the airflow and thus downforce. Pitch, roll, and heave due to load transfer also changes the ground effect and ride height of the car. The combination of these external factors lead to unequal downforce generated by the left and right sides of the car.

4.2 Simulation-to-Manufacturing Geometry Mismatch

Simulations are run with the assumption that the surfaces are smooth and true-to-CAD. However, unexpected issues can arise during manufacturing that might lead to defects in geometry. As aerodynamics is sensitive to geometry, this will lead to the manufactured car performing differently to the simulation.

5. Design Methodology

5.1 CFD Geometry Preparation

The CFD model is designed in solidworks by simplifying the full CAD of the car. This simplification includes the removal of small details with minimal impact (such as fasteners) and simplifying geometry (such as removing mounting components). Such simplification reduces mesh complexity and computational cost, resulting in faster, more efficient simulations.

5.2 CFD Simulation Setup

We use Siemens StarCCM+ for CFD.

CFD is utilized to simulate the car’s aerodynamic performance in straight-line and cornering situations. Simulations provide numerical values to quantify the performance of the aerodynamic package, and allow us to visualize the surface pressure of and airflow around the car.

5.3 Simulation Data Processing

Simulations run until 3500 iterations. Matlab is used to average the last 300 iterations to get the required aerodynamic parameters.

6. Design

6.1 Front Wing

6.1.1 Mainplane

The mainplane of the front wing generates most of the downforce. The NACA 632615 airfoil profile is used for the car, modified to fit manufacturing.

6.1.2 Flap Design and Airfoil Selection

The flaps enable the wing to reach a higher effective camber and those downforce by delaying flow separation. Since different airfoil profiles excel in different tasks, the intermediate flap may not necessarily be the same profile as the last flap.

The R26e utilized curved flaps, which improved cornering performance but introduced a high degree of complexity in manufacturing. Thus the flaps were re-designed to maintain performance without needing to be curved.

From literature review, the Wortmann FX63-137 was the most optimal choice for the intermediate flap, while the Selig 1223 was the best choice for the final flap.

6.2 Rear Wing

6.2.1 Element Geometry and Slot Gap Optimisation

From cornering results, we found that the optimum length of the second and third element to be 240mm and 110mm respectively. Additionally, a slot gap of 16.5mm between the second and third element was found to be ideal for flow attachment to both the elements.
